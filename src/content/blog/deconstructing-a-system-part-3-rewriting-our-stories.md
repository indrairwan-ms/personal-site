## I: Individuality Is Not The Problem

When I started this three-part series (read [Part 1](/blog/deconstructing-a-system-me-vs-we) and [Part 2](/blog/deconstructing-a-system-part-2-the-capitalism-cataclysm)), I knew that the posts could make it seem like I'm advocating against individuality. To make it clear: no, I do not think the problem lies in being our own person and pursuing our individual happiness.

The truth is, many of us leaned into individuality because older systems failed us. Communities could be rigid. Families could be suffocating. Traditions didn't always make space for diversity. Power within groups went unchecked with little to no accountability, and conflict was avoided — in the name of maintaining harmony — rather than confronted.

Over time, people learned that proximity to community came with emotional risk: shame, surveillance, and moral judgment. And when those same communities were weaponised by religious institutions, cultural gatekeepers, or family structures that demanded conformity over care, the exit felt not just reasonable, but necessary. So of course people opted to go at it alone.

To be fair, individualism does have its merits. It offered us the autonomy to define our own values and the safety to walk away from toxic dynamics. It gave us permission to breathe and to create outside of the collective. However, the tragedy of the modern era is that we've mistaken isolation for independence. The pendulum has swung to its furthest end. As we explored in Part 1, this swing from *we* to *me* isn't new. But we may be approaching our own Asabiyyah moment of collapse.

The challenge now isn't to return to the rigid systems of the past, but to build a new belonging. Something that preserves our individuality while re-learning the essential art of being responsible for one another.

But the question is: how do we course correct?

## II: The Collapse Of A Fantasy

Despite the "red pill, blue pill" discourse being hijacked by right-wing grifters, the metaphor from the Wachowskis' film still holds.

It's a daily choice that we make as a society. Do we stay in this somewhat comfortable fantasy, or do we choose a messier, more treacherous path?

In the past, the stories used to build and maintain this system have been palatable enough to swallow. They allowed us to live a relatively frictionless existence. But as the engine of capitalism croaks and sputters, it threatens to wake us from our deep slumber.

When that happens (and it will be sooner than we think) we have to ask ourselves, do we create new chapters of the same story? Or do we write an entirely new book?

## III: The Scripts We Live By

The process of creating systems is not done solely by bricks and law. It involves creating narratives that are palatable enough to be adopted by most people. In other words, what kind of stories need to be told for us to believe in the system?

There's the one that says we are too chaotic, too uninformed, and too fragile to govern ourselves, so we must relinquish power to a centralised authority that claims to know what's best for us.

There's the one that says choices are too messy, and that freedom is better expressed through the products we buy than the influence we exert.

There's the one that says safety can only be bought through compliance, and our greatest strength lies in following a prescribed formula, rather than in our capacity to write a new one.

These stories are mere variations, and they serve a similar purpose: to concentrate power upward and keep us from imagining otherwise.

And so we've achieved the ultimate systemic irony. As a society, we are too independent to ask a neighbour for a cup of sugar, but entirely dependent on a fragile global supply chain to deliver it to our door.

## IV: The Stories We Tell Ourselves

What systemic stories have we integrated into our identities? Probably too many to even distinguish from what we call our "self".

We tie our identities to our jobs. We define ourselves by our media consumption and our favourite celebrities. We put labels on ourselves based on the labels we wear. Almost every aspect of our personhood is tethered to a system designed to bind us. We offer our best selves to the market in exchange for a sense of belonging as fickle as an algorithm. Our hobbies and beliefs become data points for an audience. Our worth is measured by our productivity, visibility, and marketability. We fully integrate with a system that discards us the moment it has fully extracted our value. And when that happens, we will post about it proudly on LinkedIn.

We have mastered the art of performing on a stage that is already crumbling. And oh, how it's crumbling.

The old world order is collapsing. The climate is changing in ways that are already reshaping daily life. Unhinged world leaders are threatening to drag us into larger conflicts. The system is showing its seams.

So if it all crashes tomorrow — the playlists, the social media, the job, the bank account — can your story still be told?

## V: Becoming Our Own Authors

This is what the series boils down to. After deciding that this system doesn't work for us, how do we actually deconstruct it?

We don't need to burn the library to write a better book. Using familiar structures, we can design a different outcome. We can shift from systemic dependence towards intentional interdependence within curated communities.

The philosopher Martin Buber described two fundamental ways of relating to the world: *I-Thou*, where we treat others as full human subjects worthy of genuine connection, and *I-It*, where we reduce them to objects, or means to an end. The capitalist-individualist system has trained us into a world of *I-It* relationships. Your coworker is a competitor, your neighbour is a stranger, your community is a market segment.

The work ahead is learning to relate again as *I-Thou*.

Here's a framework that might help get us there.

![](Autonomy+with+Interdependence+\(1600+x+768+px)+(1600+x+1200+px).webp)

**1. Reclaiming the Self**

The first step is to disentangle identity from the system. Try this: describe yourself, then remove your job, your social roles, and any hobbies tied to consumption. Does the description still hold up? It needs to. Our core narrative must be about our personhood, not our value as a brand or a resource to be extracted. The self that survives a systemic collapse is the only self worth building.

**2. Replacing the Market with Proximity**

We must depend less on solutions handed down by corporations and authorities, and look instead to our immediate surroundings.

Economist Elinor Ostrom spent her career proving what capitalism denied. That communities are perfectly capable of governing shared resources without privatisation or top-down control. Her research showed that when people are in genuine relationship with one another and with what they share, they tend it well.

This is already happening. Solidarity economy movements around the world. Mutual aid, neighbourhood tool "libraries", childcare cooperatives, community land trusts, time banks, shared gardens, all are quietly building the infrastructure of a different way of living. These aren't utopian experiments. They are working alternatives, reducing reliance on the corporate-government loop and moving toward localised resilience.

**3. Designing a New Belonging**

If old communities were rigid and shame-driven, the ones we build next must be different by design.

The Southern African philosophy of Ubuntu (*umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu*, or "I am because we are") offers a starting point. It posits that your full humanity is only realised *in relation* to others. That's a fundamentally different premise than either the suffocating collective of the past or the lonely individualism of the present.

Practically, this means building frameworks for healthy conflict and creating ways to disagree without discarding one another. It means replacing rigid traditions with negotiated boundaries, and consent-based structures where being part of a group protects your personhood rather than erasing it.

**4. Weaving Interdependence**

The ultimate realisation of this new story is that independence is a myth; autonomy is the actual goal. Self-sufficiency is a lonely, impossible standard. We are all dependent on something. The choice is simply whether we depend on a faceless system or on each other.

We are only truly free when we have a community that holds us through our failures. Without that safety net of human relationships, we are just fuel for a system that sees us as a resource to be spent.

## VI: A Redux

Civilisation has always been a collective project. That's where this series began.

We may have spent the last few decades perfecting the art of standing alone. But the work of the coming years will be learning, or relearning, how to stand together. The myth of the self-made individual is already fading. In its place, we have the opportunity to build something far more resilient.

The system has offered us its version of the story. It enchanted us, and for a while, it was enough. But the stage is crumbling, and the script no longer holds.

So, it's time we stop being a character in their narrative. We were never just a consumer, a brand, a productivity metric, or a data point. We are someone's neighbour. Someone's person. Part of something that was always meant to be shared.

It's time to be free.

> "The only way to deal with an unfree world is to become so absolutely free that your very existence is an act of rebellion."
> 
> — Albert Camus

And none of us are free until all of us are free.
