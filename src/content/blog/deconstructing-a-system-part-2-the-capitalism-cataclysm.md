## I: The Architecture Of Isolation

Why does the system we live in push us towards hyper-individualism? It is because the system itself is inherently fragile. It is built on principles fundamentally incompatible with a natural way of life. To survive, the system must convince us that we need it more than it needs us. A true community stands in the way of that dependency.

So, the system began to market our individuality back to us as our most precious asset. To protect this individuality, we were told we could rely only on the marketplace for our emotional and physical survival.

## II: The Price Of Losing The Village

Humans evolved to be around others. The stress of living without a "village" — a group of people you trust and rely on — can be physically, mentally, and emotionally taxing. It can put you in a state of fight-or-flight, triggering your central stress response system. This puts your cortisol levels at a low, chronic and constant simmer, causing you to feel permanently exhausted but unable to truly relax. Your body is stuck in survival mode even when you're just sitting at your desk.

When you're constantly under stress, you would naturally look for a remedy. And would you believe it? The system that created the problem is also offering you a solution! Since you are already enmeshed in the preciousness of your individuality, why not seek security in it, too? That's how, in the absence of a tribe, your personal brand becomes your only shield. You feel that if you can just become unique enough, successful enough, or aesthetic enough, you will finally be safe.\

How do you do that? How do you become enough of anything? Through the magic of capitalism.

## III: How Capitalism Started Vs How It's Going

Capitalism, as it is now, is a centuries-long process of stripping away collective security. It began with the Enclosure Acts in the 16th and 17th centuries, when the lands that villages shared for survival were fenced off for private profit. This forced people off the land and into cities, turning them from self-sufficient community members into dependent wage labourers.

Then, the Industrial Revolution took hold, and the natural way of living was replaced by the clock and the factory. Survival became tied to individual output rather than communal sharing.

The most aggressive shift occurred in the late 20th century with the rise of neoliberalism. Leaders like Margaret Thatcher and Richard Nixon championed the notion that the market is the only fair way to organise society.

> "There is no such thing as society. There are individual men and women, and there are families."
> 
> — Margaret Thatcher

These leaders worked to dismantle unions, social safety nets, and local institutions, ensuring that individuals would have to turn to the market rather than to their neighbours for basic human needs. They even convinced the world to follow suit. By removing the safety net of the "we", the market ensured that the only way to catch ourselves when we fall is to pay for the privilege.

When the internet age came around, this structure was finalised. Tech platforms and their algorithms perfected the art of keeping us isolated on our devices. We are told we are "brands" and that we need to carve out our niches. Through social media, we are conditioned to compete not just with those closest to us, but also with a global, curated standard of individual success.

And, like most magic, it is all an illusion.

## IV: Buying Back What We Lost

We are pushed toward hyper-individualism because a person who stands alone is a person who must buy everything. It creates the loop that benefits the system perfectly. Individualism provides the cultural engine that makes the gears of a capitalist economy turn more efficiently, while capitalism creates the material conditions that make being an individualist feel like a necessity for survival.

This creates a tremendous burden on the individual.

We are taught that success results from individual effort, talent, and hustle, which leads us to view others as competitors rather than collaborators. Everything, even joy, becomes a point of comparison. When some of us don't get what we deserve (equitable pay, access to resources, healthcare, etc.), the system conveniently blames us for not working hard enough. Sometimes, we even do it to each other.

*"Don't you want to earn big bucks like me?"*
*"I don't understand why you don't pull yourself by the bootstraps?"*
*"You're just lazy, not like me"*

The belief that you get what you work for justifies vast wealth gaps. If you are struggling, it is framed as a personal failing rather than a systemic issue.

Even something as intrinsic as our identity and personality is not spared. To express our unique selves, we need to spend, spend, and spend even more. Our identity becomes a never-ending project to be curated through a series of transactions. Our "true self" becomes something to be discovered in a shopping cart, a subscription service, or a lifestyle aesthetic. By tying self-expression to consumption, the system ensures that we never truly reach a finish line. Our hobbies and passions fuel more mindless consumption, and we are told to monetise them. There is always a new trend to adopt, a better version of ourselves to purchase, and a deeper sense of inadequacy to fill with the next buy. We end up in a state of permanent precarity, where we are so busy maintaining our individuality that we lose the ability to organise, to rest, and to simply *be*.

This effect ripples and multiplies in ways deeply embedded in daily life. By framing success and failure as purely individual outcomes, capitalism can reduce overhead costs, such as social safety nets. Everything from childcare to emotional validation — things once provided for free in a village — now requires the market.

As collective benefits like pensions or public healthcare are scaled back, individuals have to prepare for sick days and retirement on their own, funnelling more money into private financial markets.

When workers view themselves as independent brands or sole proprietors, they are less likely to join unions or demand collective raises. If your coworker is your rival for a single promotion, you are less likely to share information or support them, keeping the power firmly in the employer's hands. We lose the power of collective bargaining, leaving us only able to hope that self-serving politicians will make policy changes that can benefit us (spoiler alert: not going to happen).

Think of any issue that we've been facing for the last 20-30 years. Climate change, pollution, nature degradation, urban decay, the loneliness epidemic, the mental health crisis, declining infrastructure, loss of third spaces, and even the lack of walkable cities. Every single issue can be traced back to this capitalist trap.

The best part? We keep getting convinced that capitalism will eventually work. Well, it does. But only for the billionaires who profit from the loneliness they've helped package and sell back to us.

## V: So, What's Next?

I've seen it many times before. People who spend thousands on bags to fix their identity crisis. People who flaunt fat cheques while managing chronic burnouts. People who travel from country to country to find themselves, only to find mounting credit card debt. It's not their fault. They're looking to solve real problems: anxiety, stress, insecurity. In the current structure we operate in, capitalism can deceptively feel like the only solution. So, what's the alternative?
